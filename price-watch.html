Why storage prices spiked in 2026

A running explainer on the AI-driven storage shortage, kept current as the market moves.

Updated September 2026

The short version

AI training and inference infrastructure created simultaneous demand for two storage tiers that used to compete for budget one after another: high-capacity nearline hard drives for hyperscale datasets, and fast NAND flash for active training pipelines. NAND manufacturers, still cautious after an oversupply period in 2022–2024, deliberately kept production flat — so when hyperscalers came back for capacity in 2026, there wasn't slack in the system to absorb it.

ADATA's chairman told press in July 2026 that DRAM contract prices would rise another 20–30% and NAND another 35–40% in Q3, on top of the 70–75% NAND jump already seen in Q2 — supply-chain guidance from an actual memory maker, not a forecast. As Q3 has played out, Bank of America's channel research (Sep 19–21, 2026) confirms DRAM contract prices rising 20–30% quarter over quarter as guided, while NAND came in more moderate — up more than 15% QoQ rather than the 35–40% ADATA flagged, with consumer demand hitting an affordability ceiling even as AI server demand keeps supply tight. Hyperscale buyers have already locked in higher contract prices for Q1 2027. The first sign of a pause has come from the consumer side: TrendForce's Sep 9, 2026 NAND bulletin says a second round of smartphone price hikes has pushed buyers back, leaving Q4 contract prices flat, while spot prices keep drifting down — 512Gb TLC wafers sat at $20.083 on Sep 14, off 0.31% on the week. Flat is not cheaper, though: prices are holding at this year's highs, not falling back. The squeeze has reached consumer external drives too: a Seagate 24TB external hard drive that sold for $279.99 in January 2026 is now $649.41 at retail — a roughly 232% jump in under a year — and Western Digital's external lineup shows similar increases, so this isn't one brand passing on costs.

Why hyperscalers pivoted to QLC flash

When high-capacity hard drives became scarce, some hyperscalers shifted workloads onto enterprise QLC SSDs (flash storing four bits per cell) as a substitute — which absorbed a large share of the QLC production pipeline and tightened supply further for everyone else, consumer buyers included.

When does this ease up?

Industry reporting points to 2027 as the earliest realistic relief, once new fabrication capacity comes online. Until then, expect continued volatility rather than a steady climb — prices can move sharply in either direction as contracts reset.

What this means if you're buying

  • Don't assume a price you saw last month will still be there — check current pricing before you commit.
  • If you need storage now for active work (not speculative future-proofing), buying now is reasonable; prices are not expected to fall in the near term.
  • Be skeptical of unusually low prices on high-capacity drives during a shortage — see our note on counterfeit capacity in the flash drive guide.
